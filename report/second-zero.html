The answerNo entry signal survived. One and a half positions did.

Every entry rule, exit rule, filter, wallet-following scheme and combination tested today came back negative, and most came back negative for a reason nobody expected going in. The failures are not near misses. The best realizable rule anywhere in the sweep is about two and a half percentage points short of paying its own fee.

One and a half positions in this market have positive expectancy, and neither is an analysis. Both are about where you stand in the transaction rather than which coin you pick:

  • Launching the coin and selling inside the minute pays +39.7% on stake before fees, on 3,347 creators, with no valuation assumption needed. In SOL rather than percent that is +8.92 an hour to the best operator in thirty recorded hours and 0.17 in total to the median one — weigh it against those. It is the rug, it is genuinely available, and I will not help build it, but that is a decision for you to make on the merits rather than one this page makes for you by word choice.
  • Being inside the launch bundle looked like it paid about +11% per SOL — and a final audit found that figure is a marginal price, available only to a wallet whose cohort holds still. Priced as one book, which is what it is, the seat pays +1.86% and is negative on the holdout. See below.

The thing that would make the second one pay is a rule for choosing which launches to bundle. One exists — bundle size is predictable before the block at Spearman 0.51 out of sample — and it still does not pay, because it buys you 0.27× of headroom and costs you 0.19× of worse fill. The entire edge lives in one block position and a new entrant does not get it.

What did survive as a usable artefact is a pair of detectors. One is arithmetic an honest trade cannot violate, and it flags manufactured volume with no false positives anywhere in the corpus; the other is a running count per creator, and a red team cut its headline from 0.94 to 0.82. Neither becomes a position. Together they cover half the money being taken here.

The median trade is −2.11% at every horizon, and −2.11% is exactly the round-trip fee. Own cross-check, 5,096 clean coins

That single line is the shape of the problem. The median pump.fun launch never trades above its own three-second price again, so the median outcome is that you pay the toll and nothing happens. Shortening the hold walks the mean toward the fee floor and never through it. With perfect foresight of the peak, only 38.2% of trades finish above water.

Second three is when STS decides. By then six tenths of everything that will ever be staked has arrived, and the price it set is the price you pay. Every other agent hit the same wall from a different side: 83.5% of graduations complete inside second zero, 92.3% of first insider buys land in the launch’s own ten-millisecond bucket, and the perfect-exit ceiling at a twenty-second entry is four tenths of one percent.

A new entrant is marginal by definition: you land behind the incumbents and lose 8.5%. And bundling every launch blindly is negative on the holdout — −0.0087 on 08-16 against +0.0336 on 08-20, a gap that is pure selection, because 13.9% of 08-16 launches attracted a bundle against 30.5% on 08-20.

This is where the whole day closes on itself. There is a real, robust, replicable edge at second zero, and it is almost exactly consumed by what the seat costs. The only version that pays is one that picks which launches to bundle — and that selector is precisely the thing twenty agents spent today failing to find.

I went looking for it anyway, inside the seat, on the 6,043 third-party second-zero positions that have a real observed fill price. Entry multiple is non-monotone. Creator history is non-monotone. One thing is clean and monotone: how many other wallets are in second zero with you — +9.6% alone, +12.2% with two or three, +27.0% with four to seven, +32.3% with eight or more, non-overlapping at the ends. And it is the single variable you cannot observe when you commit, because you submit into the launch block without seeing who else did. The only thing that selects within the seat is the number of other people taking the seat with you.

One tension I could not resolve in the time, and would rather name than bury. The 1.40× break-even above is a hypothetical — what a given entry price would return on the average coin. My own pass looks at wallets that actually filled at each multiple, and they do better: the 1.3 to 1.5× band returns +16% rather than break-even. Those answer different questions, and the agent’s is the right one for a new entrant, who does not choose their multiple by choosing a coin — they get whatever the queue gives them. But the gap is large enough that anyone acting on this should re-derive it before believing either number. (Mine is a quick pass: no null, no holdout split, more generous exit convention. Read the ordering, not the levels.)

2. It is a closed box, and only the fee is certain

Three independent routes — summing trades, inverting the curve from price, and a token-side ledger — agree that money conserves inside the window to within 0.18% of gross inflow, and 98.93% of individual launches conserve to under 0.0001 SOL. Only 1.13% of launches ever escape to an outside market, and 72 of 85 of those were already finished at second three.

So before fees this is exactly zero-sum, and after fees it is negative-sum by the fee. That much is solid. Where it goes is not, and a completeness critic caught me asserting otherwise.

I retracted this claim, and the retraction was wrong. Both corrections are worth reading.

The published figure was 92.39 kept per 100 staked, of which 5.63 to the creator, 2.8× what the exchange takes. A completeness critic pointed out it is a mark-to-model, and I agreed: recomputing with every unsold bag valued at zero, the creator’s take came out at −3.50 per 100, ROI −14.01%, and I wrote that the claim was unsupported and straddled zero.

The lesson is the sharpest of the day. Marking every unsold bag at zero felt like the conservative choice. A conservative assumption is still an assumption, and it needs the same drop-test as a filter.

3. Every signal is real, and every one has the wrong sign

This is the part that keeps catching people out, including me this morning. The filters work. They separate coins that move from coins that do not, by factors of ten and twenty-five, and they replicate out of sample. They simply have no idea which way the move goes, and the coins with the best signal dump the hardest.

  • The best social bucket returns −12.3%; buying every coin returns −9.0%; the worst social bucket returns −6.6%.
  • The wallet-graph shortlist separates 2× coins tenfold and returns −8.06% against −6.87% for buying blind.
  • Insider-flagged coins reach 2× at 6.5 times the base rate. Matched on busyness, the flag is worth −3.8 percentage points.
  • More new buyers arriving after second 20 predicts a lower price, monotonically, out to twelve hours.

Order flow predicts the peak at z = +10 to +15 and predicts the finish equally strongly in the other direction. You cannot short a bonding curve, so a working volatility detector converts into a coin flip, and the fee eats it.

The sharpest version of this came from the agent that threw everything in at once — 184 features, three model families written from scratch, nested day-block cross-validation, ~4,400 configurations. Two of its models disagree in a way that explains the whole project:

  • The ridge, targeting mean return, reaches Spearman +0.41 by ranking dead coins highest. Its ceiling is −3.6%: the cost of a round trip on a coin that never moves.
  • The logistic, targeting probability of profit, reaches AUC 0.77 to 0.90 by ranking live coins highest. Those average −12.8%.
  • A classifier trained directly on “will a +20% take-profit fill” reaches AUC 0.907 and lifts the fill rate from 5.0% to 52%, a tenfold lift. It returns −0.44%.

The models are not weak. They are strong on the wrong axis. Perfect foresight at the top 2% would return +166%; the combined model reaches −1.0%, about 4% of the distance from the base rate to the oracle.

What worksTwo detectors, no model, no training

Both are available at the three-second decision point. Neither is fitted. One is a law of the curve; the other is a running count.

The curve identity catches manufactured volume with no wallet list at all

Every pump trade event must satisfy the constant product vsol × vtok = k. Across 21 months of full-block history:

2024-10 .. 2025-11    77,305 trades      0 violations
2026-02-18            13,483 trades    132 violations   122 of them one operator
2026-08-19            14,152 trades    169 violations   140 of them the same operator

Not one violation in 77,305 trades, then it breaks. Remove that operator and the maximum peak multiple in the entire history corpus becomes exactly 14.70× — the true completion multiple of the curve. Nothing legitimate has ever exceeded it.

On the live captures the same phenomenon is caught by an equivalent rule — any printed price below the coin’s own launch price, a physical impossibility — at 100% precision with zero false positives in 6,032 clean coins, holding at 100% on the 08-16 holdout. That account touches 14.9% of the corpus, 53.7% of every coin reaching 2× and 69.9% of every coin reaching 5× — both figures reproduced independently.

The awkward part, which decides what any of this is for

Even taken at its strongest, knowing a creator will not rug does not give you anywhere better to put the money. Coins by creators who have never dumped are the dead ones — 1.4 SOL of outside money each against 16.1 for the repeat dumpers — and buying them loses as much. The red team confirmed the trading side by its own route and found it worse than first reported: on the holdout, buying everything returns −10.48%, and filtering to “the creator has not sold by second three” returns −16.42%.

You lose either way. The rug is not what takes your money; the fee and the exit liquidity are. So this is not an edge for the person holding it, and the honest framing of the whole deliverable is a warning label for other people.

What I’d doThe recommendation

Stop looking for the buy-side trade. Thirty-one agents, four waves, and three of the loudest claims retracted by the last, every family of idea I could construct, and the closest anything came to break-even was a rule that beat a stopwatch and lost to doing nothing. That negative is no longer a failure of effort or imagination; it is a property of a closed box with a fee in it.

On the two positions that do pay. The creator side is real and the arithmetic is not in doubt, and the specific cohort that earns it is the two-thirds who sell their whole holding on their own buyers inside sixty seconds. That is the rug, and I am not going to help build it — trading around ruggers is one thing, being one is another. I have measured it because the question was whether an edge exists, and the honest answer to that question includes this.

The bundle seat is the more interesting no. It is a genuine, replicable, well-controlled edge, the seat is open, and it is already bid to within two percent of break-even by people who got there first. Buying in blind is negative on the holdout. It would only pay with a rule for choosing which launches to bundle — which is the same selector that failed nineteen different ways this morning.

There was one open thread. It closed while I was writing.

If the thing that pays inside the seat is how many others are in it with you, the next question is whether that is predictable before the block. It is. Bundle size has clean 5× to 12× gradients on three things you know at launch:

  • Tweet age — under 15 seconds old gives a mean bundle of 1.57 and a 12.2% chance of four or more wallets; over five minutes gives 0.29 and 2.2%.
  • Tweet reuse — the first coin on a tweet gives 1.38 and 10.8%; the eleventh or later gives 0.15 and 0.9%.

And then a final audit found the biggest error of the day — in the seat itself

The first twenty agents were red-teamed twice. Waves two and three were not, and they produced the loudest claims. So I sent a critic at the whole thing, and it found something none of the twenty-eight had:

Three more corrections from the same audit

The late fresh swarm is decoration and I should have caught it. Its definition contains a sixty-second lookahead — “never sold in the window” is not knowable when the swarm lands. And the coordination does nothing: the control nobody ran is simply 20+ SOL in a single second after second 10, which gives 139 coins reaching 2× at 43.2% against a 3.68% base, and splitting those by wallet count gives 38.5% / 61.5% / 43.2% — flat and non-monotone. It is the SOL, not the swarm. The honest 139-coin population loses at every latency, and “5 of 6 reached 2×” is a tautology: in four of the five the price was already above 2× at the close of the swarm’s own second.

“The fee is the entire story” is true of the median and false of the mean. Blind buying is −6.58% gross with a median of −0.26%. On the median coin the fee is everything; on the mean, the market takes 6.6 points before any cost exists. That matters, because it closes the obvious rebuttal: a fee rebate does not turn this positive. And there is no escape hatch — of 11,112 trades carrying a fee field, the 1,704 that pay exactly zero all belong to the fabricating account.

“The real holdout” is a single 89-minute session. 08-16 runs 20:57 to 22:26 UTC, continuous, 1,393 clean coins. Every “it replicates on the holdout” on this page is a replication on one Saturday evening. That is harmless for the thirty negative results — reusing a holdout inflates false positives, not false negatives — but it is the only out-of-sample evidence behind every surviving positive, and this page has been treating it as a week.

Finally, in SOL rather than percent — and that changes what both survivors are

Percentages carried this entire report, and percentages are what let the seat look ten times bigger than it was. The last agent priced the two remaining positions as books, in absolute terms. It also found that the rule-miner’s impact model was wrong-signed — it displaced the exit reserve multiplicatively, which credits your own buy with cushioning your own crash, so bigger size looked cheaper. Corrected, it returns exactly the −2.110975% fee floor at any size when the reserve is unchanged.

The avoidance stack — the most durable result in the sweep — has a capacity of about 0.02 SOL per coin, and that is the finding rather than a caveat. Its strongest rule refuses coins that already pumped, so it retains the illiquid half by construction: median retained reserve is 0.200 SOL against 5.393 for what it refuses. And a coin with reserve R cannot fall further than (30/(30+R))² − 1, so the median retained coin physically cannot lose more than 1.32%. Where this actually ends

I closed this sweep three times and was wrong twice. What finally closed it was not another test coming back negative — it was running out of methods. Twenty agents fitted models. Five applied different lenses. Three applied the last ones I could name: an exhaustive rule search that beats any model at finding pockets, a direct map of the exit window, and simply reading the files nobody had opened.

I closed on a statistic here — that the real corpus produced fewer profitable-looking rules than a shuffled one — and a red team withdrew it. An independent miner with its own feature set got the opposite sign, and the null it sits in spans a median of 6 and a maximum of 1,722. It has no resolving power. The verdict it decorated still stands on the sensitivity ladder: as the support threshold rises the best rule becomes statistically significant and simultaneously worthless — +0.43% at p = 0.001, then −0.29% at p = 0.0005. Real, replicable, and 2.4 points short.

And the last audit is the reason to trust that. It was pointed at the least-checked and loudest claims, and it found the biggest error of the day in the largest positive number on this page, retracted a mechanism I had promoted, and named a method — capacity accounting — that none of twenty-eight agents had applied. The right time to stop is when the checking stops finding things. It did not, so I kept going.

I said “nothing left untested” too early. Five things were — all five now answered.

Every one of the twenty agents worked the same way: build per-coin features, fit, score a slice, test against a null. That is one method applied twenty times, and saying it exhausts the space was overreach. Five genuinely different angles were still open. All five came back, and every one is negative — but two of them found things the first twenty could not have:

  • Forward position in the block — answered. It is a capability, not a lottery: independent operator teams hold their position across launches at rho +0.82, replicating on the holdout at +0.58. But it is bought with size and reaction speed, not with tweet lead — and on the launches where a lead exists there is no bundle left to be early to. Detail below.
  • The other side of the trade — answered, and it is no. There is no mean reversion in this market at any horizon from three seconds to twelve hours. Of 312 matrix cells, eight have a positive mean and none clears the fee. The shape is the opposite of reversion: the flat bucket is the best cell in every panel and both tails are worse. Coins that fell hardest do not bounce — they keep falling, just more slowly. Detail below.
  • Twelve hours — negative. After graduation — unanswerable. Of 1,468 hypotheses at the twelve-hour horizon none survives correction, and the best gross edge anywhere is a third of the fee. The post-migration price is simply not in this corpus: a grep across every capture file returns zero hits for the venue. Detail below.
  • Reading the winners one at a time — and it found something. A late fresh swarm: eight or more wallets first appearing in the same 10 ms tick after second 10, each buying half a SOL or more and never selling. Six coins in 12,089. Five reached 2×, four reached 5×. Invisible to every regression run today. It is also a sub-second latency problem, not a prediction problem. Detail below.
  • The wallet graph and market regime — both answered, both no. The network is genuinely real: 120 communities, structure far above a degree-preserving null, membership persisting nine days. It pays nothing once you control for how many repeat wallets showed up — a community whose coins reach 2× at 13.5% against a 5.4% base has a stratum-adjusted p of 0.86. And no time cell crosses zero: every spread sits inside its own shuffle.

“I tested one method exhaustively” is not the same claim as “there is no edge”, and this page was making the second on the strength of the first. The second wave cost about an hour and produced the day’s only genuinely new mechanism, a fourth data trap nobody had found, and the retraction of a number I had repeated from the critic without checking it.

The one thing that beats chance is refusing coins — and it stops at the fee floor too

Every single one of the bottom 25 rules contains the same condition: the coin had already pumped within three seconds. The sharpest returns −43.97% on 152 training coins and −34.26% on the holdout — and 0 of 2,000 shuffles ever produced a rule that bad (p = 0.0005; the worst noise ever managed was −21.68%). It holds at every depth and every support threshold.

Stacked into an avoidance rule chosen on training days only, it lifts the holdout from −7.750% to −2.433%, refusing half the book, replicating on all four days — slightly better than the fitted model’s −3.3%.

And then it stops. The interval’s upper bound is −0.84%, so break-even is excluded; the median retained coin returns exactly −2.111%; and 48% of the retained book sits within 0.3 points of the fee floor. The drop test explains why: the refused set is winner-rich. 3.2% of refused coins return above +50% against 1.8% of retained, and the single strongest condition discards 69 of the 124 big training winners. It buys the fee floor by refusing the crashes and most of the moonshots together. It converges to the fee floor from below and stops there.

And the first direct price on the second-zero seat

The transaction-cost file had no mint overlap with the coin corpus, which killed the obvious join and was where an earlier agent stopped. But it has 100% signature overlap with its own event log — 11,125 of 11,125 — and that yields fee against trade size:

  • Trades landing in the launch slot itself pay a median 305,000 lamports against 105,000 for later slots — about 3×, and 4.5M against 1.0M at the 90th percentile. That is the first measured price anywhere in this sweep on the seat that pays.
  • 41.5% of launches pay zero priority fee; only 3.9% of early trades do. Creators do not race. Buyers do.
  • Priority fee as a share of trade size is a median 0.061% — two orders of magnitude below the protocol fee. It is not the binding cost.
  • And the protocol fee is now a measured constant rather than an assumption: 0.9500% on 96.9% of 69,732 historical trades. The 1.90% round trip is real.

One more trap it caught: the recorder’s own shutdown statistics report 84–94% of messages failing, which is an artifact of counting failures before the deduplication check. The trustworthy figure from full-block data is 6.6% to 22.6%. Anyone reading those stats naively is wrong by an order of magnitude.

And know what the survivors are worth before deciding anything. The avoidance stack is the most durable finding here and it earns −0.42 SOL an hour at a small clip; it has no positive size. The creator position is the only robust positive and the best operator in thirty recorded hours made +8.92 SOL an hour from it, while the median one made 0.17 SOL in total. Whatever you decide about the ethics, decide it against those numbers rather than against the percentages.

Build the detector instead, if you build anything. Two rules, no model, no training, no fitting. One is arithmetic that cannot be violated by an honest trade; the other is a running count per creator. Together they flag 36.1% of launches and cover 52.6% of the money being taken. Nobody else appears to have the first one — flux’s own decoder already self-checks the fee identity and passes it 100%, and it does not check the curve identity, which is the one that catches this.

Two honest caveats on that. It protects other people, not you; there is no version of it that turns into a position. And the certainty and the reach live in different rules — the one with no false positives reaches 2.2% of the money, and the one that reaches half the money is the 0.82 that a red team just marked down from 0.94.

And if you ever capture again, these six fields are the difference between answerable and blind. Every one of them cost this sweep something specific — agents re-briefed mid-run, tests declared impossible, numbers reconstructed instead of measured. Ordered by what they unlock:

MethodWhat to trust, and what I found wrong

Every agent worked read-only on the real captures, corrected the corpus the same way, priced own-order impact from the curve state at both ends, priced stops at the close of the second they triggered rather than at the level, graded every timing rule against a matched-exposure stopwatch, corrected for multiplicity, ran a shuffled null, and scored 08-16 exactly once. Several ran a leakage positive control — injecting a banned future field and confirming the score jumped.

Corpus defects found today, all of which affect earlier work

  • 116 mints appear in more than one file. 12,205 lines are 12,089 coins, so anything that concatenated the seven files double-counted about 1%.
  • A silent 1000× units break on the first three days. It does not look wrong if you miss it, because peak-over-entry ratios are scale-free.
  • The recorded files are an older schema than the current recorder. outcome.complete, gapSec, observedSec, feeBps, whoCapped and the candle reserve fields do not exist. Hygiene rules written against them are not buildable.
  • social.nth resets every recorder session and matches a causal count on only 83.3% of rows. It was load-bearing in the social work.
  • The verdict’s “seven hour-matched windows” are not hours. They are 5 to 8.5 minutes each, 43.8 minutes of chain time in total. The hour-matching is real; the duration is not, and no trend should be read across seven short samples.
  • The capture corpus is 30.0 recorded hours, not seven days — 340 clean launches per recorded hour. A rule trading the top 2% would fire 163 times a day, not eleven times a month. This is a firehose, and it matters for what any strategy’s numbers mean.
  • A fourth trap in the twelve-hour tracker, found on the last day of the sweep. Because its running high and low are reset to 1 when it adopts a coin, the first trade after second 60 retroactively records a “crossing” of every ladder level between the sixty-second price and the three-second price. 1,170 of the 1,569 rows with any crossing — 74.6% — contain at least one crossing that never happened. One coin sitting at 0.13× its entry records five levels crossed in a single second it was never near. The agent that found it had 35 profitable cells in its first run; correct fills took that to zero.
  • Only one day is a real twelve-hour observation. 08-20 has a median watch of 43,541 seconds; 08-21 was cut short at ~9.3 hours; and 08-16, the designated holdout, tops out at 319 seconds — it cannot hold out a long-horizon question at all.
  • The recorder ran in 50 disjoint sessions, so hour-of-day is a day grid in disguise: only 7 hours appear on more than one day with n ≥ 30, each dominated by a single day. This corpus measures the recorder as much as it measures the market, and that bounds any time-conditioned claim.

A number I repeated without checking, and it was wrong

The completeness critic reported a monotone ~5-point gradient in expectancy by the launch rate in the preceding minute — −3.66% at the quietest against −8.51% at the busiest — and I recorded it as “real but does not cross zero”. Re-derived properly, it exists only in one outcome universe, does not appear at all in the four days that have candles, and — decisively — the within-day null’s median spread is 5.577 points, larger than the observed 5.305. p = 0.5607. The direction was right and the size is exactly what shuffling produces. I should have tested it before repeating it.

A hard ceiling on what this data can ever settle

Worth stating plainly, because it bounds every selective result above and below. Per-coin net return has a standard deviation of 37 to 43 percentage points. To establish a genuine +2.12% edge at 80% power you need about 1,900 trades. Which means:

This page says the flagged account touches 14.9% of the corpus, while FINDINGS.md reports 15.5% and 17.2% from two independent routes. The figure quoted on this page is the strictest of the three, which is the right one for it to be.

Three numbers that were wrong and got caught

  • An exit agent charged own-order impact into the recorded reserve, as if its own buy had evaporated from the curve. I challenged it, derived the correct treatment, and it found the bug: round-trip impact on a constant-product curve is exactly zero at an unchanged reserve, at any size. Every number moved 1.5 to 5.7 points.
  • The strongest-looking feature in the project, initialBuySol, was a bounded-downside artefact. On a curve the price cannot fall below launch, so it is where on the curve you buy. Controlling for entry price collapsed ΔR² from +0.011 to +0.00003.
  • My own closed-box headline. I asserted the creator takes 5.63 of every 100 SOL staked and could not support it; the honest answer is a bracket that straddles zero, and the fee is the only certain term.

And one kill I should not have banked

The published shortlist feature this whole plan rested on — 1.07% to 12.15% reaching 2× — was reported as vanishing once the manipulation account is removed: one coin in 141 against zero in 427. The critic checked the arithmetic. At the without-actor base rate of 2.24% you expect 3.17 hits in 141, and P(≤1) = 0.173. Observing one is entirely consistent with the effect being unchanged. The correct word is uninformative — which is exactly the word this document used for a 68-coin lead it kept. I held the retraction to a looser standard than the finding.

The same critique named a mechanism worth taking seriously: removing that account is a population change, not a control. After removal the clean arm produced zero 2× events where the recorded rate predicts 9.6 (p = 0.0001), so it is a population with much less outcome variance — and that could turn a real effect into a null.

A later agent drop-tested it directly and the objection does not hold. The filter removes coins whose buy-and-hold return is −11.68% (median −34.08%) and keeps coins at −8.59% (median −2.25%). Excluding the account makes the universe less extreme, not more — so it cannot be manufacturing the negative results. It is hygiene, not a selection rule. Good criticism, and it was worth checking rather than assuming either way.

One identity that collapses several “different” signals into one

Worth recording because it changes how to read the whole scorecard. On a bonding curve, net flow between two moments is sqrt(k·p₂) − sqrt(k·p₁), and the square root is monotone. So “net SOL flow turned negative” and “the price fell” are the same event — 100.0000% agreement across 211,120 coin-second pairs. Contrarian-on-flow and contrarian-on-price are not two ideas, and several signals tested separately in this sweep were literally the same signal under different names. On a venue with an order book they would be genuinely distinct; here the curve makes them identical by construction.

What the sweep never asked

The critic’s lead, and it was fair: twelve agents asked which coin to buy and not one priced the other side of the trade, even though the document’s own conclusion named that side as the only profitable one. I sent two agents at it, and both gaps are now closed — the creator side is priced above and the bundle seat is priced above that. Between them they produced the only two positive numbers of the day and the correction to my own retraction, so the criticism earned its keep.

It also flagged that the corpus is not 12,089 independent launches — 817 tweet ids are reused across 4,955 coins and one tweet spawned 116. Cohort position as an entry rule is dead, and a cluster bootstrap by handle puts the design effect at 1.04×, so the intervals here survive the dependence.

Its third suggestion — add social.handle as a second identity axis to lift the detector’s coverage from 62.5% to 75.3% — I sent to an agent, and it is apparent coverage only. With the window-closed rule the gain is 6.9 points, not 12.8, and on exactly the coins the wallet axis is blind to the handle predicts nothing: AUC 0.5322, p = 0.29, precision lift 1.03×, below what a field with no behavioural content reaches on the same coins. statusId adds no coverage at all and scores 0.41, worse than chance. The reason is the interesting part: handle-to-creator is not one-to-one, and the top handles are imjustaguyfun at 116 coins across 114 different wallets. The handle identifies whose tweet got scraped, not who deployed — it covers because tweets are recycled and cannot predict because the person it names is not the person who dumps. Ship on the creator wallet alone.
